This is my personal speculation, and even if you read to the end, it may turn out to be nothing more than a delusion.

Residents are the ones blocking data centers, but I think the ones who gain more the more they are blocked are the companies that already own data centers.

My father has worked since 1994, 33 years now, as an electrical engineer building data centers at the construction arm of a group famous for semiconductors. Thanks to that, I grew up hearing about what a data center is long before the AI boom. I know the data centers running AI today are built differently from the ones back then, but my interest started back then. When I went home on weekends, I often heard about big overseas cloud companies coming into Korea, and that made me think for a long time about how this industry works. This time I checked that thinking against the numbers.

## Once they were the future

Only a few years ago, data centers were called a future growth engine, and plans to build them came out somewhere in the world almost every day. Then at some point the mood changed. Citizen groups and residents objected over noise, electricity bills, and jobs, and the number of projects actually being cancelled grew.

## Are they really being blocked? The numbers

![Bar chart of the scale of delayed and cancelled US data center projects. $130 billion in Q1 2026, $68 billion in Q2](/images/20261021-datacenter-incumbents/01-지연-취소-규모.webp)
<small>Source: Data Center Watch</small>

But there is something odd. Over the same period, data center capacity under construction in the US actually grew 24.8% to 7,481MW, a record high. The number being blocked is a record, and the number being built is also a record. It is closer to "new entrants are getting blocked" than to "data centers are shrinking." In Korea too, the builders are busy. SK and AWS have started building the largest AI data center outside the Seoul metropolitan area in Ulsan.

## The reasons for opposition we can see

| Reason | What can actually be confirmed |
|---|---|
| Electricity bills | The capacity price on PJM, the largest US power grid, jumped from $28.92 per MW-day (2024/25) to $329.17 (2026/27), and an independent monitor's analysis says data centers account for 63% ($9.3 billion) of the 2025/26 increase |
| Noise and environment | Concerns about cooling equipment, emergency generators, and electromagnetic fields from high-voltage lines. Protests continue near residential areas in the Seoul metropolitan area, such as Doksan-dong in Geumcheon-gu, Seoul |
| Jobs | The criticism that they get large tax breaks but employ few permanent staff |

![Bar chart of PJM capacity prices. $28.92 in 2024/25, $269.92 in 2025/26, $329.17 in 2026/27 (per MW-day)](/images/20261021-datacenter-incumbents/02-PJM-용량-가격.webp)
<small>Source: PJM auction results</small>

These reasons are all real. But I don't think they are the whole story.

## My view: who smiles the more it is blocked

Let me draw a line first. For people who build data centers, the more that get built, the more work there is. What I suspect is not the builders but those who have already built and earn from leasing. Even in the same industry, the two have opposite interests.

From the standpoint of a company that already built data centers and earns from them, new data centers springing up everywhere is not welcome news. When supply grows, rents fall and profits shrink. Conversely, when new construction is blocked, the capacity they already hold becomes more precious.

The numbers are moving in exactly that direction.

| Item | Figure |
|---|---|
| Vacancy rate in major North American markets | Record low of 1.4% (even though supply grew 33.7% in a year) |
| Northern Virginia vacancy rate | 0.3% |
| Share of capacity under construction already leased | 80.4% |
| Rents | Rising in every size band (CBRE) |

![Horizontal bar chart of data center vacancy rates. Major North American markets 1.4%, Northern Virginia 0.3%, Atlanta 1.0%, Dallas 1.8%](/images/20261021-datacenter-incumbents/03-공실률.webp)
<small>Source: CBRE 2026</small>

A CBRE representative also said, in effect, that this is the first industry they have seen where vacancy keeps falling even after supply triples. In a market like this, a new competitor being blocked means incumbents gain pricing power just by sitting still.

There is one more thing. Connecting to the power grid is first come, first served. The Uptime Institute, an industry research group, reported that existing data centers are reserving far more power capacity than they actually need in preparation for future growth, and that this is blocking other users from connecting. Even without resident opposition, it is a structure where whoever lined up first holds the spot.

## What I honestly could not confirm

The strongest version of my thinking is "the incumbents are stirring up opposition like a cartel," and I could not find evidence for that.

| What I looked for | Result |
|---|---|
| Suspicions about funding of opposition groups | There are some. But the ones pointed to are environmental groups and overseas foundations, not existing data center companies, and NPR noted there is almost no direct evidence even for those claims |
| Groups with unclear funding sources | Two opposition groups in Mason, Michigan each reported the other as their only donor, so it is impossible to know where the money really comes from |
| Reports that incumbents funded opposition | Not found |

So this is as far as I can go for now. I don't know whether anyone is blocking on purpose. But who benefits from the blocking continuing can be seen in the numbers. If the funding sources of opposition groups start to be disclosed transparently, I think we will be able to tell whether this thinking was right or wrong.

I'm curious who you think benefits most when new data centers get blocked.
