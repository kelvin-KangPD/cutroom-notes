I made the 2-minute film played right before the groom's entrance at our wedding with Higgsfield. I barely shot anything; most of what's on screen is AI-generated images and video.

The ceremony was on September 19 at 1 p.m. When the film ends, the on-screen me says "Everyone, please turn around," and at that moment the real me walks in through the back door. Work ran from August 27, when I saved the storyboard, to September 6, when I generated the last clip. About eleven days.

The finished film is on YouTube. I trimmed the black screen after the ending down to 5 seconds.

Watch the finished film on YouTube

0:53 in the final cut. The low-angle bench shot. The hand offering the water is my wife's (AI-generated)

The generation process was long, so I split it into four parts. Part 1 is why I made it, the opening shot, and the prep. Part 2 is the workroom and wandering scenes, part 3 the bench scene, part 4 the wedding hall scene and the final edit. I'm keeping as much as I can: how many times I rewrote prompts, which models I used and how many clips, and where I got stuck.

Frames from the final cut in order. I left out the real photos and videos from the montage inside the eye

No performances, no wedding song, so I bet on the first shot

The reason I made this film is simple. Our wedding had no performances. No wedding song either. We needed at least one thing to entertain the guests.

The film also includes our dates. But stitching date footage together is guaranteed to be boring. The goal was to show our dates and still make the guests feel "Oh, that was fun."

People don't really care what video I make or what dates we went on. They check whether it's fun first. And I thought that gets decided in the first shot. So the most important decision in this film was what to open with.

After a lot of picking, I went with a parody of the motorcycle scene from the Korean film "Beat." The one where he rides at night and spreads his arms wide. It wasn't in the storyboard; I added it to the very front later.

Left is the original from "Beat," right is 0:09 in the final cut (AI-generated). Same angle, same streetlights, only the face is mine

I made this shot on the night of September 5 with Higgsfield Genjutsu motion transfer. I put in one motorcycle riding clip and one photo of my face, and the prompt was a single line: "Replace the face of the person in the video with the reference image." 720p, 12 seconds.

Genjutsu motion transfer. One face photo and a one-line prompt swapped only the face

I picked it on two criteria.

Criterion Why
A scene many age groups know Guests of every age sit in a wedding hall
Fun and surprising even if you don't know it People who don't know the original should still instantly notice "Is this AI?"

The captions "Me, lonely" and "I want to be happy" pass over the motorcycle, and that lonely guy cuts to his workroom desk, where the actual story begins. Looking back, this one scene seems to have carried the whole film. I haven't properly captured how the guests reacted yet. I tried a cross-cut with the hall footage once, but apart from the first burst of laughter the reactions didn't come through well. The guest reaction footage the video company shot for us hasn't arrived, so when it does I'll re-edit the reactions and post them here.

The story starts by asking AI about happiness

I kept the plot simple from the start. Kang PD asks an AI how to be happy, follows its advice and heads outside, wanders through odd places, meets someone who offers him water on a park bench, and walks into the wedding hall.

Scene Location What happens Storyboard length
S#1 Workroom (night) He asks the AI, "What's the surest way for a person to be happy?" The answer: "Go outside. When you meet your true match, something will flash before your eyes!" 0:00–0:15
S#2 Cafe → park → moon → snowy mountain → summer street The face stays in the center of the frame while outfits and backgrounds keep switching. At the end he flops onto a bench, exhausted 0:15–0:40
S#3 Park bench A water bottle comes in from the side, he looks up, the backlight clears and he sees my wife. A fast zoom into her eye, where a montage of real photos and videos plays 0:40–2:00
S#4 Wedding hall waiting room From that expression it cuts straight to him in a tuxedo, talking to the guests on a selfie cam, then at the door: "Please look behind you" 2:00–2:20

The storyboard put camera, direction and sound for every cut on one sheet. I stacked S#1 through S#4 vertically into a single PDF called "Kang PD pre-ceremony film final integrated storyboard sheet." While working I called it a pre-ceremony film, but it actually played mid-ceremony, right before the groom's entrance.

S#1 workroom. I even wrote down the lines for the AI chat screen and the sound effects

S#3 is the climax. The tilt-up from the water bottle to my wife's face, the backlit lens flare, the Ratatouille-style zoom into the eye, and the montage inside take up the most space in the storyboard. This scene also ate the most credits and time.

S#4 is the part that connects to the real venue. The on-screen me talks into a selfie cam in the waiting room, lowers the camera, and when the film ends the real entrance music starts.

The storyboard ran 2 minutes 20 seconds, and the final cut's picture ends at 2:15. I planned the montage at 50 seconds but it got much shorter, and the lines changed too. How they changed is in parts 3 and 4.

Two Claude accounts and Higgsfield

I used two AI tools together. Higgsfield for generating images and video, Claude for designing prompts and reviewing results. On Higgsfield I used my current account from the start.

Claude started on my wife's account. It understood my scene descriptions so well that I ended up making my own account and upgrading to Max. That account move later became a big hole. The conversations from her account didn't carry over to mine, so I made a handover document and pasted it in, but that document kept only the conclusions and dropped all the reasons. I write about this separately in part 4.

I connected Higgsfield to Claude as a connector. Claude generated images directly, pulled up generation lists, and checked my balance and transaction history. Convenient, but it cost a lot.

What happened Cause What I changed
One conversation used 4% of my Max usage, and a few more messages pushed it to 7% The Higgsfield connector has about 80 tools, so their descriptions rode along every turn even when unused. Results like 100 transactions also kept piling up Turned the connector on only for generating, and did planning and script talk in a new chat with it off
Claude could pull up generation lists but couldn't see the pictures The lists only returned IDs and URLs; Claude couldn't open the images themselves I pointed out which slot on screen held the image to pick

On Higgsfield I made a "wedding film" project and started uploading material on August 29. That day I began with the S#1 workroom.

Locations as elements, faces by angle

Higgsfield lets you save locations and characters as elements and call them in prompts with an @ sign. I made location elements for the wedding hall, snowy mountain, moon, workroom, summer street, park and cafe. The snowy mountain and moon went through several versions, up to @bg-snow-5 and @bg-moon-4.

Seven location elements and one character element saved in Higgsfield. I replaced the home background in the character photo with a solid color

I kept one work order to the end. Make the location image first, make an image with the person placed in that location, then animate that image into video. If I threw in the location and person separately at the video stage, they didn't match, while an image given as the first frame went into the video as is.

Faces took even more trial and error. My first character elements (@char_jungu, @char_sumin) were based on a single photo, so the faces came out weak. So I added more photos to the elements and also fed photos directly as references per cut. This time I assumed more photos would be better. It was the opposite.

Photos of my face uploaded to the project on August 29. I shot profile, 45-degree and front separately. The inside of my home was visible, so I replaced just the background with a solid color

Putting front, side and head-down photos in together averaged each angle and the face got mushy. So I changed the rule.

Priority Photo to use as a reference
1 Exactly one photo taken at an angle close to the shot you're making
2 If there isn't one, one good front shot and one good profile
Never Front, side and looking-down photos all at once. All three directions get averaged at the same time

This rule wobbled once with my wife's face. She had three photos: front, turned 45 degrees with eyes forward, and 45 degrees with eyes lowered. I needed her face looking down from my point of view on the bench, so Claude suggested using only the third one, closest in angle. I figured her eyes were lowered in that one so they wouldn't come out right, and put in all three. A rule is a rule, but I also had to weigh the expression in each photo.

I also pinned facial features down in text. I wrote out eye spacing, eyelid shape, nose bridge width, cheekbone height, jawline angle, philtrum length and lip thickness, and ended with Do not average these features. That sentence was meant to stop the averaging I got when mixing angles.

If I open the character element I still have, @강준구 holds 7 photos. A mix of suit photos from our wedding shoot and everyday photos.

The @강준구 element. One main image and six extra photos

Credits don't roll over

My Higgsfield account is on the Plus plan. 1,000 credits come in on the 3rd of every month at 11 a.m. Korea time. I had Claude page through the transaction history to the very end to check, and on the August 3 record there was a line reclaiming 843 leftover credits right next to a line granting 1,000 new ones. Leftover credits don't carry over to the next month; they disappear.

So right before the reset there was no reason to hold back, and right after it I hesitated over even one video. The per-run cost by model, from the transaction history:

Model Type Cost per run (credits)
Nano Banana Pro Image 2
GPT Image 2.0 Image 0.5–8.5 (17x difference depending on settings)
Cinema Studio 4.0 Video 20–72
Seedance 2.5 Video 19.26–132.73

On August 31 I grouped the latest 140 transactions by model: of 563 credits total, 8 videos took 356 (63%). Nano Banana Pro made 38 images for just 76 credits. The 132.73 credits gone in one shot was, by its timestamp, the final Seedance 2.5 Edit 1080p run at 10:41 p.m. on August 30 (Korea time). By the transaction history I spent close to 300 credits that day alone.

The 140 transactions I pulled on August 31, summed by model (drawn from the transaction history myself)

From August 30 to September 6 I generated 36 videos on my account. By model:

Model Count Main scenes
Seedance 2.5 20 Bench POV tilt-up, eye zoom-in and zoom-out, wedding hall selfie cam
Seedance 2.5 Edit 9 Wandering scenes (swapping background and outfit on real footage), adding a bow tie to the hall selfie cam, the shot pulling out of the eye
Cinema Studio 4 Three talking shots in the workroom, one bench POV tilt-up
Higgsfield Genjutsu 2 Face swap (motion transfer), adding a bow tie (object swap)
Seedance 2 1 Walking up to the bench and sitting down

Of the 36, 19 were 480p test runs and 16 were 1080p. I checked composition and motion at 480p, then reran only the good ones at 1080p with the same prompt. Credits vary a lot with resolution, so this order settled in naturally.

Things I missed in prep cost me a lot of credits and time later. If I did this again, I'd go in this order.

What to do Why
Write down "whose POV, looking at what" for every cut in the storyboard I only wrote "water bottle POV," and Claude read it as the bottle's point of view. It was my POV looking at the bottle
Keep decisions and their reasons in one file When I moved accounts I handed over only a summary, and every reason vanished
Make 480p test → 1080p a rule from the start One 132-credit run costs as much as six test runs
Turn the connector on only for generating The tool descriptions alone ate a lot of Claude usage every turn
