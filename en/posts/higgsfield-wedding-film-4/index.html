The AI-acted selfie cam talked so cheesily and smelled so much of AI that I couldn't use it. So I did the acting myself and left only the clothes and the location to AI. In the last scene I arrived again at the same conclusion as the wandering scene in part 2.

10 seconds into the final selfie cam shot. AI put the clothes and location on a video I actually acted myself

Part 1 was the opening and prep, part 2 the workroom and wandering, part 3 the bench. Part 4 runs from 1:48 to 2:15, where the picture ends. In a tuxedo, I talk to the guests in the wedding hall lobby, put the camera on the floor, and finish with "Everyone, please turn around." When the film cuts off, the real groom's entrance begins.

In the original storyboard, at the end of the smiling face on the bench (#11), I planned to cover the lens with my palm to black out the screen and then cut to the wedding hall. I dropped it. Nearly two minutes in, one more device would drag. I judged that a match cut straight from the smiling face on the bench to me in a tuxedo hit harder.

I drew how the camera would move in the hall directly on a photo I took at the actual venue.

Scene 4 blocking. ① is the handheld selfie cam, ② the camera set down on the floor

Camera What it does
① Selfie cam Filming my own face with the camera at arm's length while I talk to the guests
② Floor After talking, I lower the camera and set it on the floor. It faces the wedding hall door

The drawing didn't stay a drawing. In the end I put it in as a reference image just as it was.

Why I dropped the AI selfie cam

Since it's a match cut, the front half, the smiling face on the bench, had to land first. This smile image needed both the expression and the face right, so I kept generating while switching models: GPT Image 2, Nano Banana Pro, even Seedream 5.0 Pro. For one Seedream run the whole prompt was "Too chubby... slim it down."

Image history for the night of September 2. Almost the same composition, with only the expression and face changing

Once the smile was set, I moved the same face to the wedding hall. I gave Seedream 5.0 Pro two photos of me in a suit and a photo of the venue lobby as references, and wrote one line: "Change into a black suit and change the background to this place."

Seedream 5.0 Pro. The face smiling on the bench came straight into the wedding hall lobby

The expression and face angle carried over from the bench smile almost unchanged. That's how well the two halves of a match cut have to line up. Only the tie came out white, so I put the result back in as a reference and corrected it: "wearing a black bow tie instead of a white tie." This became the first frame of the AI selfie cam.

Only the tie changed (AI-generated)

From that first frame I made a Seedance 2.5 selfie cam. At first it was just talking into the camera at arm's length. Next, to add the move from ① to ② in the storyboard and drawing, lowering the camera and setting it on the floor, I wrote out how the camera and body move at each moment: "one continuous shot from a single camera," "as the camera is lowered it turns to face the other way," "it rests on the floor with the lens tilted slightly up."

1 and 6 seconds into the AI selfie cam (AI-generated). Shoes and a door stand in front of the lowered camera

The movement worked. The problem was the talking. The selfie cam made with Seedance talked far too cheesily, and it reeked of AI. Rerunning it, I decided there was no saving it and dropped it. AI can make a convincing face and clothes, but at least on this project it couldn't keep up with acting that talks into a camera. To show how cheesy it was, I plan to upload the failed video separately and attach it here.

I act, AI does the clothes and location

The final shot started not from a person AI made, but from a selfie cam video I acted myself.

I shot the selfie cam at home on my phone. Shooting on a GoPro would bring wide-angle distortion. So I shot on the phone but acted as if it were a GoPro. Haha.

The mouth shapes as I talk, the head movement, even the hand lowering the camera to the floor are exactly what I did. The AI only had to change the clothes and the location. Still, it got stuck in a few places.

Where it got stuck Why What I changed
The lapels came out shiny satin I wrote "tuxedo with satin lapels" in the prompt myself, so I got exactly that "A plain black suit, all matte wool, no satin, no sheen, no contrasting lapel trim"
The link from the smiling face on the bench to the wedding hall With the palm blackout gone, it had to go straight from the eye to the hall Put an opening inside this shot that starts in an extreme close-up filling the frame with the eye and pulls out over the first second
Where to stand The design drawing already had the spot drawn Gave the design drawing as a reference and wrote that the figure in it is only a position marker and never appears on screen

I also wrote that the face and hair should follow the reference photos, not the source video.

The first try was Genjutsu's object swap. The wedding hall lobby and tuxedo went on well, but the lapels were satin.

Genjutsu object swap (AI composite). The lapels picked up a satin sheen

After that I moved to Seedance 2.5 Edit and added the sentences in the table above. I put in the design drawing with the yellow figure exactly as drawn, and once I told it in words that this was a position marker, the drawing didn't show up on screen; only the position was set.

1 second into the final version. The design drawing with the yellow figure went in as a reference as is

With the pull-out from the eye inside this shot, placing it right after the eye zoom and real montage from part 3 gave the link from the eye to the wedding hall. The match cut was finished at the generation stage.

0:01, 0:03 and 0:17 of the final version. Pulling out of the eye, talking, setting the camera on the floor

Compared with the AI-only version, the ending is different too. In the AI version shoes stood in front of the floor camera. The final follows the real movement of me setting the camera down, so only the door shows beyond the floor. And the lines became mine: "I think 'opened my eyes and found myself at the wedding hall' is exactly the phrase for this," "Really, a flash, like light," "I'll go in now," "Everyone, please turn around."

The finished shots were joined in Premiere. The monitor screen from part 2 was an HTML recording composited in, and the problem of leaves not covering my wife in part 3's high angle was solved by laying real leaf footage over it. I worked on two monitors: the program monitor on the left screen, the timeline on the right.

The program monitor on the left screen. At 1:53, subtitles sit on the last selfie cam shot

The final timeline on the right screen. Five video tracks, seven audio tracks. The playhead is on the Scene 4 selfie cam

It played at the 1 p.m. ceremony on September 19, right before the groom's entrance; the on-screen me said "Everyone, please turn around," and the real entrance followed.

A handover document with only the conclusions

Besides the AI models, there was one more place I got stuck: the handover. As I wrote in part 1, I started Claude on my wife's account and moved to my own. The conversations didn't come across accounts, so I pasted a handover document the previous chat had summarized into the new chat. Continuing from that document, so much was missing that work couldn't move.

The cause was that the document kept only the conclusions and dropped every reason. Only after having Claude reread the entire original conversation (253,348 characters), saved as a Word file in Google Drive, could I see what was missing.

What the document had The reason only the original conversation had
"Use 3 photos directly as references" With many photos, each angle gets averaged and the face falls apart. So a photo at an angle close to the shot is best
"#11 palm blackout deleted" Nearly two minutes in, one more device would drag. A match cut instead
#07 bottle POV Not two shots but one continuous 12-second shot, with a rack focus passing from the bottle to my wife

The Claude in the new chat couldn't tell from the document why it was "deleted," and following the document's "3 photos directly" for face references would have gone against the conclusion I remembered. I couldn't find the face reference conclusion right away even in the original, so I fixed it by telling Claude again what I remembered.

I had the same problem of re-explaining every time the chat changed in my blog work too. Now I keep the rules and state in a GitHub repository outside the chat and have each new chat read it first. I wrote about that in the post about building an AI brain on GitHub.

Making it with AI was not the easy road

I don't really regret anything about the film itself. I did what I could, the reaction was good, and I learned a lot about filmmaking. So instead of "if I did it again," here's what I'd take with me if I make something new.

What to take Why
Talking scenes from video I act myself, from the start The AI selfie cam talked cheesily and reeked of AI, so I dropped it
Specify the material of clothes from the start I wrote "satin lapel tuxedo" and got satin
Use design drawings directly as references Tell it the figure is a position marker and it doesn't appear; only the position is set
Connected shots inside one shot Putting the pull-out from the eye inside the selfie cam shot finished the match cut at generation
Handovers with conclusions and reasons together Work couldn't move with a document missing the reasons, and I had to reread a 250,000-character original

Next time I make something, I want to use Edit mode better and make something far better than this.

Making video with AI was absolutely not easy. It may have been a harder road than shooting it myself. I didn't start it to make things easy in the first place. That's why I think it was a meaningful challenge.

The dates in this series are only the ones with records. In reality I'd been planning on and off for at least a month before. I revised the storyboard so many times I lost count. It took a lot of thought, time and effort. Honestly, as you go, "just pay a company to do it…" slips out on its own.

But what I thought while making it points the other way. With AI you can try so many things. If the composition is off, rewrite it in screen proportions; if the face falls apart, swap the reference and generate again right away. On a real set you wouldn't dare. For video makers, more paths have opened.

And the fundamentals I've built doing video carry over to AI video, of course. In this film too, writing the storyboard, splitting the bench scene into three cameras, choosing a match cut over a palm blackout, and joining it all in Premiere at the end were all things I already did. What changed is that the limits of space came off. Snowy mountains, the moon, a wedding hall lobby: I can make them into scenes without going there. So I think the stronger someone's fundamentals, the more they can shine in AI video.

I had to study again doing this. I had to properly learn the shot terms and film grammar I'd picked up over people's shoulders. High angle, low angle, tilt-up, rack focus, match cut, bust shot, even lens angle and distance: only by naming them exactly in the prompt did I get close to the shot I wanted. The one word "water bottle POV" throwing off the bottle and hand was, in the end, a terminology problem too. There's so, so much to learn. It's an excuse, but that's why there are plenty of scenes I'm not happy with.

One aside. I thought it might be worth automating the very thing people pay companies for and turning it into a service. Because the barrier is pretty high. I've been working hard with AI and have made a fair number of videos, and doing this all the way through, there still seemed a long way to go. Which means many people still can't do it. I smelled money. Heh.

Trial-and-error log

Bench smile images (September 2, 10:41 to 11:44 p.m.): first GPT Image 2 (quality Medium, 1344x752). Between 10:50 and 10:53 alone, 10 images came out in three minutes. Then Nano Banana Pro with a one-line "a lovely smile," and three more with the same English prompt. Following the principles set in part 3, the prompt first pinned all directions to the camera, then split into paragraphs.

Item Prompt content
First line All directions relative to the camera; frame left is the viewer's left
Posture Torso turned toward the camera so the shoulders nearly face the lens, back straight, both hands on the thighs
Gaze Just beside the lens, very slightly off to frame right. Meeting the eyes of the person sitting there
Expression Mouth closed, corners clearly lifted, crinkles at the eyes. The face of having found what he was looking for
Face Eye spacing, eyelids, nose, cheekbones, jawline, philtrum and lip thickness exactly from the reference photos. Do not average these features; don't slim the face or smooth the skin
Frame 50 mm, 1.5 m away, seated eye level, bust shot cut at mid-chest, 35 mm film texture

In part 3, #08 kept the body facing front and turned only the head. #11, the reverse, turns the body toward the camera too, because the surprised moment has passed and he's answering the person sitting across.

11 video: on September 3 at 0:17 (480p), 0:28 and 0:41 (1080p) with Seedance 2.5. From a frame filled by the eye, it pulls out fast over 2 seconds and stops on a bust shot; the blank eyes sharpen, a smile spreads, and around 3.5 seconds he says "Yes, I'd like that." The first sentence of the Genjutsu prompt: "Keep this man's performance as is. Face and identity, lip and jaw movement while talking, head movement, posture, the timing of every action." The camera too was to stay as is, down to the shake while lowering and settling on the floor.

The opening sentence of the 14:43 run: start in an extreme close-up with the eye and pupil filling the frame, and pull straight back along the lens axis over the first second. Fast at first, slow on arrival. The iris becomes an eye, the eye a face, stopping on the source's bust shot. The connection: one continuous take with no cut and no dissolve, exactly one person.
